The story

I

Training is outgrowing memory.

Single-cell models are moving to billion-cell corpora. Past the size of host memory, every cell must be streamed from storage — and the loader decides how fast training can go.

I · WHY NOW

scGPT trained on 33M cells; Tahoe-x1 on 266M. scBaseCount holds 502M cells, and Arc Institute plans over a billion cell experiments for virtual-cell modelling.

I · WHY NOW

While the corpus fits in host memory, any loader can keep it in RAM. Every inefficiency stays hidden.

I · WHY NOW

At 16 KB per cell in CSR, 266M cells already need 4.26 TB — more than the 2 TB of host memory in a DGX H200. A billion cells need 16 TB.

I · WHY NOW

Every epoch reads the corpus in a fresh random order. The loader is now on the critical path of every training step.

Corpus sizes as cited in the technical report (§1). CSR size at 2,000 nonzeros per cell with 32-bit values and indices.

II

Why is this hard?

A billion cells must be small enough to store and fast enough to read one at a time, in any order. Existing formats give up one to get the other.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

A cell-by-gene matrix stores one expression value per gene. As dense float32, one cell is 160 KB.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

Stack the cells and a typical dataset is 16 GB of dense matrix.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

A billion-cell training corpus is 160 TB as a dense matrix.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

Keep only nonzero values and their gene ids. At ~2,000 nonzeros per cell: 16 KB per cell, 16 TB in total.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

Each cell's nonzeros move to the start of its row as (value, gene id) pairs. Rows still have different lengths.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

All values go into one array, all gene ids into another, and indptr records where each cell starts. A cell is now a slice of two arrays.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

HDF5 and Zarr split each array into chunks and compress every chunk on its own. With Zarr's PCodec, 16 TB becomes about 3.3 TB.

II · HOW BIG IS IT

Its slices sit inside compressed chunks of both arrays. To get one cell, the loader fetches and decompresses every chunk it touches.

Illustration: 10⁹ cells × 40,000 genes × 4 bytes; 2,000 nonzeros per cell; Zarr (PCodec-8) at its pooled 4.79× ratio from the report's compression table. Chunk sizes are schematic.

II · EXISTING LOADERS

Top: bytes each loader stores per cell. Bottom: bytes it reads from storage to deliver one random cell. One square is a 4 KiB page.

II · EXISTING LOADERS

Zarr and Parquet store a cell in 5–7 KB, yet read 450–1,150 KB per random cell: whole compressed chunks and row groups, mostly other cells.

II · EXISTING LOADERS

memmap and H5AD skip decoding but store ~20 KB per cell, and page reads still pull in neighbours. Lance comes closest: 12 KB stored, 29 KB read.

II · EXISTING LOADERS

Smaller than every format here, and about one page of device traffic per random cell. The next chapter shows how.

II · THE TRADE-OFF

A codec finds redundancy across the bytes it sees together. Put more cells in one compressed unit and the ratio rises; at one cell per unit, general-purpose codecs barely compress.

II · THE TRADE-OFF

Reading one cell decodes its unit: about k / R(k) cells' worth of bytes. At 2,048 cells per unit, XZ-9 reads over 400 cells' worth to return one.

II · THE TRADE-OFF

Separate the unit of statistics from the unit of decoding: share models across a 4,096-cell chunk, keep every cell independently decodable.

Pooled mean ratio of raw and normalized CELLxGENE matrices by cells per independently compressed unit (report Fig. 2b); read cost = k / R(k), before page and metadata overhead (report §2.2). Data

III

The CellPack format.

Start from native CSR and change one thing at a time, each step fitted to how single-cell data looks. Measured end to end: 22.5 KB per cell becomes 2.33 KB — 9.65× smaller — and every cell still decodes on its own.

III · STEP 1

indptr locates a cell's slice in two separate arrays. With 64-bit offsets, 32-bit gene ids and 32-bit values, a cell in our sample takes 22.5 KB.

III · STEP 1

indptr cuts the indices array and the values array into one slice per cell.

III · STEP 1

A cell's ids and values move together behind a 3-byte header (flags + nnz). One cell, one contiguous record.

III · STEP 1

Up to 65,536 genes, a 2-byte id is enough, and raw counts keep their 16-bit type: 14.1 KB per cell.

III · STEP 1

Records sit end to end in a chunk; an offset and length per record replace indptr. Every later step compresses records, but never makes one depend on another.

III · STEP 1

Look up a cell's offset and length, then read its contiguous record. Neighbouring cells do not have to be decoded.

Measured on 9 CELLxGENE datasets · 147,456 cells (raw counts and normalized, 4,096-cell chunks) by re-encoding with the current writer. Data

III · STEP 2 · GENE IDS

Within a record, ids are sorted; store the difference to the previous one. A shared rANS model codes the gaps in 4.6 bits instead of 16.

III · STEP 2 · GENE IDS

Each chunk ranks genes by how many of its cells express them. A cell's genes are mostly common ones, so their new ids sit close together.

III · STEP 2 · GENE IDS

Up from 21% in source order. Gene ids drop to 3.32 bits each; one permutation per chunk restores the original ids.

Measured on 9 CELLxGENE datasets · 147,456 cells (raw counts and normalized, 4,096-cell chunks) by re-encoding with the current writer. Data

III · STEP 3 · VALUES

Expression comes from small integer counts, so even normalized values take only a handful of distinct bit patterns per cell.

III · STEP 3 · VALUES

The record keeps a small dictionary of its distinct bit patterns, exactly — nothing is rounded — and replaces every value with a rank.

III · STEP 3 · VALUES

Ranks are skewed: the common ones cost a fraction of a bit, rare ones a few bits. A shared rANS model codes them close to that ideal length.

III · STEP 3 · VALUES

2.7–2.9 bits per value instead of 16 or 32: a cell goes from 9.61 KB to 2.14 KB. The writer can also code values against a per-gene mode, where a chunk benefits.

Measured on 9 CELLxGENE datasets · 147,456 cells (raw counts and normalized, 4,096-cell chunks) by re-encoding with the current writer. Data

III · STEP 4 · SHARED MODELS

The probability tables for gaps, ranks and dictionary bytes are fitted to the whole chunk and stored once. Each record keeps its own coding state, so it decodes without its neighbours.

III · STEP 4 · SHARED MODELS

Fitting a model to each cell loses context: 15% larger even if its tables were free, 1.9× with them. zstd on each cell is 3.4× larger.

III · STEP 4 · SHARED MODELS

Tables, gene permutation, offsets and nnz totals add 38 bytes per cell — under 2% of the chunk.

Measured on 9 CELLxGENE datasets · 147,456 cells (raw counts and normalized, 4,096-cell chunks) by re-encoding with the current writer. Data

III · STEP 5 · PAGES

Laid end to end, a small record can still cross a 4 KiB page boundary — and cost two reads.

III · STEP 5 · PAGES

Records are packed so each fits inside one page; the gaps are padding. Measured cost: 151 bytes per cell.

III · STEP 5 · PAGES

Records may move physically; the offset table maps each row to its page and position.

III · STEP 5 · PAGES

A random read fetches one page and decodes one record. On disk, 2.33 KB per cell: 9.65× smaller than CSR.

Measured on 9 CELLxGENE datasets · 147,456 cells (raw counts and normalized, 4,096-cell chunks) by re-encoding with the current writer. Data. Record sizes in the animation are illustrative.

III · THE FILE

One matrix as one stream: a 4 KiB superblock, page-aligned chunks of 4,096 cells, and a directory at the end.

III · THE FILE

A 64-byte header, the shared models, the gene permutation, the offset tables — then records packed into 4 KiB pages.

III · THE FILE

The matrix X sits in the middle; obs, var, obsm and varm annotate its rows and columns. Usually X is stored as CSR.

III · THE FILE

CellPack is a general two-dimensional matrix format. An H5CP file is an H5AD file whose X is an embedded .cpk stream; everything else keeps its AnnData encoding.

IV

The loader.

A format is half the answer. The loader turns an access sequence into model-ready batches: it plans the cache ahead, reads asynchronously, decodes in parallel and delivers in order.

IV · THE LOADER

Training already knows its order: shuffled permutations, epoch after epoch. The loader receives that sequence up front. Watch one cell travel through it.

IV · PLAN

An online compiler scans upcoming accesses, finds pages and decoded rows that will be reused within the cache budget, assigns them frames and publishes plan entries ahead of the workers.

IV · WORKERS

Each worker claims the next position and follows its plan entry: use a cached frame, admit a page for later reuse, or read a miss.

IV · I/O

Page reads go through io_uring. A worker submits, moves on to other work, and harvests completions later.

IV · DECODE

A record decodes on its own; genes are mapped to the model's vocabulary; values are converted; an optional native function builds model inputs.

IV · OUTPUT

Workers finish out of order, but the output ring publishes slots in the requested order. Training leases a slot without copying and releases it when done.

Conceptual diagram of the loader's modules; not to scale.

IV · CACHE POLICY

A shared LRU cache takes a lock on every access: look the page up, move it to the front, evict the tail. While one worker holds the lock, every other worker waits in line.

IV · CACHE POLICY

Shuffled order makes history a poor guide: at a 10% budget LRU removes only 9.1% of page misses. Meanwhile the lock idles the SSD. The loader drops to 0.80M cells/s and 3.2 GB/s of reads, against 1.45M cells/s and 6.4 GB/s with no cache at all.

IV · THE PLAN

Training already knows its order. A planner on its own cores reads it ahead and turns it into one entry per position. Workers follow their entries; they never consult or update a shared cache.

IV · THE PLAN

Each entry fixes the frame, whether to admit or use, and the generation to expect. A use may run before the admitting read lands, so it waits for that generation. A frame takes a new page only after D quiet positions, when no reader of the old one can still be running.

IV · THE PLAN

The admitting access reads page 7 from the SSD once. The later cells on that page find it in f7 and skip the device. No worker asks whether a page is cached: its entry already says so.

Simulated schedule of four workers and one frame (D = 2). LRU and no-cache figures: report Fig. 6 at a 10% budget, 32 workers, one NVMe SSD, three-run means. Measurements

IV · COMPILING A PLAN

Two consecutive uses of a page, at positions u and v, form a reuse interval. Keeping the page in a frame from u to v saves the read at v.

IV · COMPILING A PLAN

Sweep the sequence and decide each interval at its second use v: keep it if a frame is free over the whole span, otherwise read the page again. For equal pages served one at a time, this is Belady's optimum with bypass: 9 reads here.

IV · WHY NOT BELADY

Belady assumes one access after another. CellPack's workers overlap and complete out of order: position 7 may admit F into f1 while position 6 is still decoding C from it. The new page would be swapped in before the old one is swapped out.

IV · WHY NOT BELADY

A worker starts position u only after every position at or before u − D has finished, so in-flight positions never drift more than D apart. A frame last used by then is guaranteed free: eviction precedes admission. The bound turns a run-time race into a rule the compiler checks ahead, with no reader counts or locks while loading.

IV · WHY NOT BELADY

The compiler keeps Belady's rule but reserves each fresh admission's frame from u − D. Here F no longer fits: 10 reads against Belady's 9. Those reserved frames are the price of running out of order safely.

IV · OFFLINE COMPILATION

With the whole sequence stored, the compiler collects every reuse interval and sweeps them by right end. A range-max/range-add tree over positions answers whether frames are free over [u − D, v]. Page groups compile in parallel shards, and the result does not depend on scheduling.

IV · OFFLINE COMPILATION

Residencies take frames in admission order, and each new tenant raises its frame's generation. Every position receives its entry: admit, use or read. The plan is bound to that exact sequence and replayed once to verify it.

IV · CACHE POLICY

At a 10% budget the plan removes 38.4% of page misses, against 38.7% for simulated Belady and 23.5% for the best classic policy. Throughput rises to 2.29M cells/s, from 1.45M without a cache.

Toy sequence: 16 accesses, 2 frames, window D = 2, decided with the compiler's page rule; the worker timing is illustrative. Results: report Fig. 6, three-run means; Belady from the report's same-protocol simulation. Measurements · Simulation

IV · WHY ONLINE

An offline plan needs the whole access sequence in hand. For one billion cells and five epochs that is five billion cell ids: 20 GB before compiling anything.

IV · WHY ONLINE

Set-based models such as STATE consume cells in sets of 512. When every cell starts a set each epoch, five epochs need 5 × 512 × 1B = 2.56T accesses: 10.2 TB of ids, 5.5× the compressed corpus and 5× host memory, before a single plan entry.

IV · WHY ONLINE

An offline plan is compiled before loading starts, so the whole sequence and every plan entry sit in memory while the workers sweep across them, and stay there after the sweep has passed. At billion-cell scale with sets of 512, that is 10.2 TB of ids alone.

IV · WHY ONLINE

Online, a planner works one horizon ahead of the workers. It generates each id from the seed only when it reaches it, compiles the entries, and the ids are dropped as soon as the workers have passed. Memory follows H, not the length of training: the billion-cell run looked one billion accesses ahead on eight planner cores and delivered its first batch 106 s after start.

Sequence sizes at 4 bytes per cell id. Corpus size, horizon, planner cores and first-batch time: report, billion-cell run. Host memory: DGX H200, as in chapter I.

IV · ONLINE PLANNING

The online compiler decides reuse only within H accesses of its frontier. A longer horizon keeps more reuse, but holds more of the sequence in memory and delays the first batch until H accesses are compiled. The billion-cell run used one epoch, so pages read in one epoch can be kept for the next.

IV · ONLINE PLANNING

At its frontier X, the compiler decides each interval that has just closed, if it is at most H long. It tracks only how many frames are in use; which frame comes later.

IV · ONLINE PLANNING

At Z = X − H, frames get names and generations. Entries are published at P = Z − D: a later admission reserves its frame from u − D ≥ P, so no published entry ever changes. Workers consume entries at F.

IV · ONLINE PLANNING

An interval longer than H closes after its start has frozen, so its page is read again. In the report's runs this costs nothing measurable: at a 10% budget the online plan removes 38.5% of page misses, the offline plan 38.4%.

IV · ONLINE PLANNING

When workers catch up with the published frontier, the compiler freezes early, trading look-ahead for latency. It never freezes past X − D − C, its chunk size C behind, so every published decision stays valid.

Synthetic stream, 3 frames, D = 3, H = 12, decided with the compiler's page rule. Online and offline figures: report Fig. 6, 10% budget. Measurements

IV · ONE WORKER

A worker runs many lightweight coroutines, one per claimed position. It never blocks on the disk.

IV · ONE WORKER

Claim, look up the plan entry, decode the record, map genes, run the process function, write the slot.

IV · ONE WORKER

The coroutine puts its read on io_uring and parks. The worker claims the next position right away.

IV · ONE WORKER

After each claim and between stages, the worker checks its completion queue. Landed reads resume their coroutines.
